# Session 18 Plan — The Shadow

**Picking up from:** Session 17 ended with the party at the threshold of Lolth's final chamber. They have the key, they have the door open, and they have not yet touched anything inside. Nothing in the sanctum has been resolved.

**Session goal:** Run the sanctum fight, get the compass, get off the island, and reach the Shadow — where the real story is, and where the party's best instinct is the worst possible move.

---

## Reference — what the party already has

Carried forward from Session 17:

- **Magical spellbook** — the cultist-scribe's journal. The party has read the last legible entry (see below).
- **Joss Tagrin** — rescued deckhand off the *Gull's Wager*, last of his crew. Not in fighting shape (speed 15, can't hold a weapon). Whether they brought him along or left him caged is a live thread.

**The journal entry they have already read — this is the load-bearing clue:**

> *"She is not waiting for the curse to break. She is waiting for it to break **twice**. The first time was the captain. The second time will be us — whoever takes the helm next. She wants the chair filled and emptied again. I do not know why. I have stopped asking. The webs answer questions they were not asked, and I would rather not be answered."*

**Lore they did NOT find** (Room 5, the Altar of the Deep Queen — still available if you want to seed it elsewhere, or drop it):

> *Sixty years ago the Spider Queen came to this island on purpose, drawn by the storm and the black ship that sails inside it. The Shadow's curse is older than her claim on the Abyss. Whoever breaks that curse will not destroy what waits aboard the Shadow — they will **free** it. She does not guard the compass to keep the Shadow lost. She guards it to make certain that when the Shadow is found, it is found by the hand she chooses, at the hour she chooses, and no sooner.*

## Scene 1 — The Sanctum (Lolth's Final Room)

The sealed door opens into a volcanic cavern, roughly 60 feet wide and 80 feet long, everything slick black rock. Three things dominate the space:

**The two wall crystals** — set into carved sconces on the left and right walls, each about 3 feet tall, pulsing violet, humming just below hearing. These are **binding seals** — they are what keeps the central crystal intact. As long as both stand, the prison holds.

**The central crystal** — A pillar of deep violet growing from the floor at the room's midpoint. There is a faint purple glow around it, protecting it. You hear a faint heartbeat from inside it.

**The altar** — There is a faint purple glow around it, protecting it. The compass rests here, magnetically locked in place by the crystal's power — it won't budge until the central crystal shatters and the tether breaks. Behind the altar is a full-size statue of Lolth, carved in black stone, her eight arms outstretched and her face a mask of fury.

**Guarding the room:** 2 stone drider statues flanking the altar — carved black rock, eerily detailed, posed mid-strike. They are easy to dismiss as decoration. They are not. The moment either wall crystal takes damage, both statues crack open and two living Driders tear free, animating simultaneously and attacking.

### The Shape of the Problem
**The sequence is forced:**
1. Smash both wall crystals and fight the Driders.
2. Break the central crystal.
3. Grab the compass (now free on the altar).
4. Get out before Lolth fully manifests.

### Destroying the Wall Crystals

Each wall crystal has **20 HP**
- It explodes in violet shards — creatures within 5 ft take **2d6 force damage**.
- The central crystal shudders. The compass glints brighter inside.
- Lolth's silhouette moves for the first time.

### Breaking the Central Crystal

The moment the central crystal shatters:

> *It doesn't break — it detonates. Violet shards scatter across the stone. The heartbeat stops. The black stone around the statue of Lolth cracks and falls away, revealing a dark, writhing mass of limbs and fangs. The altar glows with a violet light, and the compass lifts slightly off its pedestal.*

- All creatures in the room take **2d8 psychic damage** (DC 14 Wis save, half on success).
- The compass is now free on the altar — **free action to grab it**.
- **Lolth begins to emerge.** She needs **1 round** to fully manifest before she can act freely.

**Round 1 after the shatter:** She is rising — a growing darkness at the crystal's former site, limbs unfolding from nothing. She cannot act yet, but the room reacts: webs erupt from the walls, forcing all creatures to make a **DC 13 Str save** or be restrained (one action to break free).

**Round 2+:** She's free. The party will not survive a prolonged fight.

### The Escape

Through the door and down the tunnel with the compass. **Describe this:** the sealed door slams shut — then something hits it from the other side with a force that sends cracks running across the stone. The door holds. The tunnel is quiet.

Then, from the other side, her voice. Not roaring. Not screaming. Just speaking, in Abyssal, slow and even.

> *Let them run.*

Then nothing.

## The Truth — DM reference

*None of this is stated on the ship. The party learns it in Scene 7 — from Seraphel if they broke everything, from Xendros if they didn't. Read it first; everything downstream depends on it.*

### What is at the bottom of the Shadow

**Two orbs, locked together.**

- **The purple orb** is the physical manifestation of **Lolth's power** — what was cut away from her when she took the Abyss, given to the sea to drown because the sea keeps things. The old sea powers could not destroy it.
- **The gold orb** is **Seraphel**, a **male Aasimar** — a celestial who volunteered. He didn't cast a seal on it. He *became* one. He wrapped himself around her power and has held it, awake and alone, for centuries. He is not guarding the prison. He **is** the prison.

The Shadow is the hull they were sunk into. The permanent storm was raised around it to hide it and to bar gods from reaching it — that's the answer to *why the storm protects the ship*: the storm isn't protecting the Shadow, it's protecting **what the Shadow is holding**, and it is doing that by keeping her out and keeping it moving.

### What the chair does

A celestial and a piece of a goddess cannot share a hull without a mortal anchor holding the arrangement in place. **The captain's chair is that anchor** — a post, not a throne. Sitting in it puts a person into the binding's circuit, exposed, with nothing between them and the purple orb.

**Lolth takes whoever sits.** That is the "break."

**The first break already happened.** The ship's original captain — **Emerich Sallow** — took the post willingly, long ago, and she took him. Chair filled, then emptied. That is exactly the journal's line — *"she wants the chair filled and emptied again."* The Shadow has sailed crewless ever since, and the celestial has been holding on alone without an anchor, which is why the storm has been degrading for a century.

**The second break is whoever sits next and then leaves.** Two takings and his grip fails. **The ship rips itself open**, and both orbs come free:

- **Lolth's power is loose in the world**, wearing the body of whoever sat down.
- **Seraphel is released** — no longer a sphere, restored to a man, still glowing gold. He is the only person alive who can explain any of this.

### The moral shape of the session

**The only way the party doesn't cause a catastrophe is to touch nothing and leave.** No riddle solves it, no clever plan improves it, no heroic sacrifice helps. Doing nothing is the win condition, and it is *unsatisfying by design* — that's the point. Everything about the room is built to make sitting down feel like the brave, correct, self-sacrificing choice.

If they meddle, they get the story, an ally, and the worst thing that has happened to this campaign. If they don't, they get a quiet exit and no answers until Saltmarsh.

### The star chart, and who gave it to him

Session 16 established that Hex navigates by a star cluster matching no known constellation, and that he "got the information from someone." This is that someone.

**Ilvessa** *(name is a placeholder — swap freely)* **is a drider.** She was a drow priestess who failed Lolth badly enough to be remade, and being remade was not the end of the punishment — it was the start of it. She was sent back out into the world to run errands. Setting Hex was one of them.

**How she hid it — and that she failed.** She met him three times, always in the dark, always already seated behind a table when he arrived, always still sitting when he left. On the third meeting, leaving, **he looked back and saw her move.** Not all of it — a shift of something long and jointed in the dark behind the table, and too many of them, and the wrong height off the floor. It lasted about a second.

**Hex knows exactly what she is.** He has known since he was twenty-two. He has never said it to anyone.

**This is the part he is most ashamed of**, and it is worse than the chair. He took the chart anyway. He walked out of that room having seen what was under the table, and he decided — at twenty-two, exiled, with nothing — that it didn't matter where the map came from as long as the map was true. Thirty years of rationalizing that decision is most of what Hex is made of.

**What he told himself:** she was a monster who wanted a thing in the sea kept in the sea, and monsters can want good outcomes. He was not wrong about that. He was wrong about which outcome she wanted.

**The chart is not a sky. It is a web.** Eight points radiating from a center, joined by concentric rings — Lolth's sigil, drawn as a star map. It *works*; it genuinely navigates, because it is the same technology as the compass that "points by web, not by north." Hex has steered by the Spider Queen's own mark for thirty years and never recognized it, because he is a sailor and not a priest.

**What she told him about the chair** — a true statement with one poisoned word:

> *"There's a ship in the storm holding something that must not get out. It has had no captain for a hundred years and it is failing. When you find it, take the chair. It's the only thing that will hold it."*

Every word of that is true except **hold**. She never said what happens to the person.

**And then she cheated.** On his way out the third time, she said one more thing — quiet, quick, small enough to survive being overheard by whatever was listening:

> *"If you ever get down there and there's a light that's kind to you — believe the light."*

She was not supposed to say that. It cost her something. Hex has never understood it and has never forgotten it, and he will repeat it in Scene 5 without knowing he's describing the gold orb. **Let the players make that connection.**

**She is offscreen this session.** Hex describes her; nobody sees her. Expect a player to work out what she was about four sentences into the description — and remember that **Hex worked it out thirty years ago and is standing right there letting them get there on their own.** She's a fully loaded thread for later, and a possible reluctant ally, because she has been used exactly as hard as he has.

### What Hex has been hiding

**Two things, not one.**

**1. He has known about the chair for thirty years, and he intends to sit in it.**

**2. He has always known the woman who sold him the chart was a drider.** He saw her move, leaving the third meeting. He took the map anyway, and he has never told a living soul — not Maren, not Eda, not the party.

The second one is the one he's ashamed of. The first one he thinks is noble.

He never told the party either thing, because he knew they'd stop him. Which means **the blood oath in Session 15 was never about claiming a prize.** It was about binding a competent crew to the job of delivering him to that chair. He has been sincere about everything else and dishonest about the only thing that mattered, and he knows it.

When it comes out in Scene 5, he is not deciding. He is confessing.

**Consistency check.** The scribe's journal is honored exactly: two breaks, the first was the captain, the second is whoever takes the helm next, the chair filled and emptied again. The unfound Room 5 lore also holds — *"whoever breaks that curse will not destroy what waits aboard the Shadow, they will free it."* That is literally what happens.

## Scene 2 — Off the Island

The chanting has stopped.

Every cultist on the island is dead. Not killed in a fight — the moment she was free she had no further use for them. The party walks back out through a treeline where everyone is simply finished, and the rain that had fallen straight down without pause since they landed **stops** partway to the beach. That silence is worse than the chanting was.

- **She does not chase.** Nothing pursues them. Play the walk out completely quiet and let the players talk themselves into a panic.
- **Joss Tagrin** (if they brought him) is the one who says it out loud: *"Why isn't it raining?"*
- If they left Joss caged, this is where it costs them — the cage is open and empty, and there's no blood.
- Back aboard the Stormcaller, **Maren** wants a heading. The compass answers.

**DM note:** resist any temptation to add a fight here. The party just took the worst risk of the campaign and got away clean, and the wrongness of getting away clean is the scene.

## Scene 3 — The Storm Parts

The compass points, and the weather **opens.**

Hex has spent his entire adult life fighting this storm and it has never once let him through. It is letting them through now — a corridor of flat water and dead air running to the horizon, walls of black cloud standing off on either side like something holding a door.

- **Hex is elated.** Let him have it. This is the best day of his life.
- **Maren is not.** She says the quiet part: *"Six years I've plotted your courses, Captain. Weather doesn't do favors."*
- **Dov** asks whether they're the first people to ever see this. Nobody answers him.
- Then the Shadow: a black-hulled galleon under full sail with no wind to drive it, silent but for rigging. No lights. No crew. Far bigger than it has any right to be.

### The chart on the table — the earliest tell

**Put it in front of them here, before the ship.** Hex has it out and weighted down at the corners, and he's happy, and he's talking. Describe it plainly and without emphasis:

> *Old hide, soft at the folds from thirty years of handling. A single point at the center. Eight lines running out from it. Rings crossing all eight at even intervals, and stars marked where the rings and the lines meet.*

**Anyone may attempt DC 15 Religion or Arcana**, and the party has seen the source material twice — the wall carving in the sanctum, the sealed door, and the Abyssal runes Short Stacks found woven into the webs in Session 17. On a success:

> *It isn't a constellation. It's a web. You have been looking at it since the day you set foot on that island.*

If they get it, they arrive at the Shadow already suspicious of Hex, which is exactly right — and it makes Scene 5 a confrontation instead of a surprise. If they miss it, say nothing and let it sit on the table in plain sight for the rest of the session.

**Hex, asked where he got it:** he'll talk about her happily. A cartographer, three meetings in back rooms thirty years ago, always seated, gone after the third. He tells it as the luckiest thing that ever happened to him.

**He is lying by omission and it is the first crack.** He knows she was a drider — he saw her move on the way out — and he leaves that out of the story every single time he tells it. Play the telling as slightly *too* smooth: a well-worn anecdote with one worn patch in it.

**If a player asks the question — *"was she ever standing up?"* — do not let him lie outright.** Hex doesn't lie to their faces; that's not his failure mode. He goes quiet, and then he says something like *"No. She wasn't."* And he won't elaborate until Scene 5.

That's the ideal outcome: the party arrives at the Shadow knowing their captain took a map from something that wasn't a person and never mentioned it. If nobody asks, the omission just sits there and detonates later in the confession instead.

## Scene 4 — Boarding the Shadow

Empty. Silent. A century too old in every detail — rope that should have rotted, brass that should have gone green, a deck worn smooth by feet that aren't there.

**The quarterdeck.** The ship's wheel, and bolted to the deck behind it, a **captain's seat.** Empty. Heavy. Not decorative. Every line on this ship is worn except that chair, which looks like nobody has touched it in a hundred years — because nobody has.

**The captain's name is available if they look for it** — a brass plate below decks, a ship's register, the flyleaf of a logbook in the great cabin: ***Emerich Sallow, master.*** Nothing else about him survives. No portrait, no personal effects, no last entry. Whatever he was is as gone as he is, and the party will feel that absence more than any description of him would give them.

**The remains.** Three steps from the port rail, a dead man. Old bones, a mate's coat, not a captain's. **Not Sallow** — Sallow left nothing behind to find.

> *He is not lying the way a man lies when he falls. He is stretched out — reaching, one arm forward, fingers still curled around nothing. Whatever he was carrying is not here. He was crawling toward the rail, and he did not make it.*

**This is a tell and it matters later.** A **DC 15 Insight or Medicine** check reads the body honestly: he wasn't fleeing the ship, he was trying to throw something *overboard*, and he died three steps short. He was the ship's first mate. He watched **Emerich Sallow** sit down in that chair and stop existing, and he spent his last hours trying to put the compass's twin into the sea so nobody could ever find this place again.

**Below decks.** It gets older and wronger the further down they go, and the ship is bigger inside than out. The compass keeps pulling **downward** — past the hold, past the bilge, past where a ship stops having decks.

**Optional pressure while they descend** (use one or two, keep them cheap):
- Footsteps on the deck above them, moving aft, unhurried.
- A hammock still swinging in a room with no air moving.
- A ship's bell rings once, from a bell that isn't there anymore.

## Scene 5 — The Bottom of the Shadow

*This is the scene. Give it room. It is temptation, not exposition — do not explain anything here.*

> *The compass stops pulling. The space in front of you is longer than the ship is, and the far end has no far end. Chains run out of the dark into every rib of the hull, hundreds of them, and where they all converge they hold two things off the floor, locked against each other like hands.*
>
> *One is violet. It strains. Looking at it directly costs you something small that you don't notice leaving.*
>
> *The other is gold, and steady, and warm, and the light coming off it feels — unmistakably — like being looked at kindly by someone very tired.*

### Neither orb speaks

**Nothing down here talks.** No voices, no words, no bargaining. Both orbs transmit, and what they transmit arrives already inside the person receiving it, indistinguishable from their own feelings. That is the horror of the room: nobody can point at the thing that's influencing them, because from the inside it doesn't feel like influence. It feels like clarity.

**The purple orb transmits permission.** Not an instruction — it never tells anyone to do anything. It broadcasts *relief*. A settled, physical calm: the hard part is over, the answer is obvious, the obvious thing is also the good thing, and doing it will feel like setting down something heavy. It makes the room feel **safe**, which is the single most dangerous thing it could do.

**Describe the effect, never the source.** Don't say "the orb makes you feel calm." Say the argument someone's about to make sounds more reasonable than it did a minute ago. Say a character notices they've stopped being afraid and can't remember when. If a player asks whether something is affecting them: *you don't feel like anything is.*

**It doesn't need to say "sit down."** Hex walked in here already carrying that instruction — she gave it to him thirty years ago. The orb's whole job is to make him feel good about it. It has never once had to argue with anybody.

**The gold orb transmits one thing.** Wordless, warm, and pushed hard enough that it *hurts* — the way a shout hurts when someone is trying to stop you stepping into the road:

> ***No.***

That's all he can manage. He can't form words, can't explain, can't identify himself. He has one syllable of meaning left and he spends it every time somebody drifts toward the ladder. And underneath it, unmistakable to anyone paying attention: **exhaustion**, and the fact that whatever this is, it is a *person*, and it has been doing this alone for a very long time.

**This is the light Ilvessa told him to believe.**

### The tells — the chair is the trap

**Nothing in this room tells the party what any of it is.** What the room gives them is four separate reasons not to touch it. Two are free.

| Tell | Check | What it gives |
|---|---|---|
| **The chains are inscribed** | DC 17 Religion or Investigation | Worn script along the links: *the seat is a post and not a throne. It was filled and emptied once. It must not be filled again.* Blunt, unambiguous, and hard to reach — this is the one that flatly states the answer. |
| **The first mate's body** | DC 15 Insight or Medicine, on the quarterdeck | He was crawling to the rail to throw the compass's twin into the sea. He wasn't running from the ship. He was trying to make sure nobody else ever came. |
| **The gold orb's warning** | **Free, no roll** | Anyone who has seen the gold orb and then approaches the chair takes the wordless ***no*** to the chest. He can't form words. He can form that. And it is precisely what Ilvessa told Hex to believe — if the party has heard that line, this is the moment it detonates. |
| **The chart is a web** | DC 15 Religion or Arcana, back in Scene 3 | If they caught it on the crossing, they already know his star map is Lolth's sigil and have had hours to sit with what that means about who sent him. |
| **Touching the chair** | **Free** — DC 14 Wis save | On a failure, a two-second vision: a man in a captain's coat — Sallow, though they've no way to know it — lowering himself into that seat, and then not being there, and the chair not moving at all. On a success they see it and shake it off. Either way they've *seen it*. |

**Read the room, not the sheet.** If the players are clearly about to sit, hand them the free tells hard and let them decide anyway. The choice must be theirs. Do not roll dice to save them from it.

### Hex confesses

This is the emotional centre of the session. He is not deciding anything down here. He decided thirty years ago and never told them.

**Play it as relief, not as a reveal.** He is not caught — he *volunteers* it, because he's finally standing in the room and he doesn't have to carry it alone for one more minute. That's what makes it awful.

What comes out, in roughly this order:

1. **He's always known about the chair.** The woman who sold him the chart told him: the ship holds something that must not get out, it's had no captain in a century, it's failing, and taking the chair is the only thing that will hold it.
2. **She wasn't a woman.** This is the hard one, and it comes out flat, because he's rehearsed it in his own head for thirty years and never once out loud. He looked back on the way out of the third meeting and saw what was under the table. Long, jointed, too many, wrong height off the floor. He took the chart anyway. *"I knew. I've always known. I decided a true map was a true map."* If the party asked him about her on the crossing and he went quiet, this is the answer to that question, and they'll know it.
3. **He's known he was going to do it since he was twenty-two.** Every year of the exile, every deal, every course Maren ever plotted, was aimed at this room.
4. **That's what the blood oath was for.** Not to help him claim a prize. To make sure someone would be bound to get him here. He looks at whoever swore it when he says this.
5. **And the last thing she told him** — offered up almost as an afterthought, because he's never known what it meant and he's never stopped repeating it to himself: *"if you ever get down there and there's a light that's kind to you, believe the light."*

**Then let the table sit with it.** Point 5 is the loaded gun, and there is a light in this room that has been kind to them since they walked in.

**Note what point 2 does to point 5.** Hex has spent thirty years treating the drider's advice as reliable while knowing she was one of *them* — and the one piece of her advice he's never been able to use is the only piece that was ever meant to help him. He is standing in a room where both halves of that finally resolve, and he cannot see it, because the purple orb has him feeling good. Do not connect it for them. Do not have Hex connect it — he genuinely doesn't, because the purple orb has him feeling *good*, and a man who feels good doesn't re-examine his plan.

**If a player connects it out loud**, that is the single best moment available in this session. Give it full weight: Hex goes quiet, looks at the gold orb, and for the first time in thirty years considers that his one lucky break might have been a hand on his shoulder.

**He still asks them.** Even now — maybe *especially* now — he asks, which he has never done in the entire campaign. He genuinely wants to be talked out of it and cannot ask for that directly.

**Play him honestly and sympathetically.** He is not a liar for sport. He assembled a genuinely noble plan out of real courage, real guilt about Saltmarsh and his sister, and information that was planted in him on purpose. He is completely wrong, and the players have to talk him out of the best thing he has ever tried to do.

**But don't soften point 2 to protect him.** He isn't a dupe — he's a man who saw the shape of the thing under the table and chose the map. Everything since has been him proving to himself that he chose right. If the party is angrier about the drider than about the chair, that is a completely fair read and he has no defence for it.

**If they're furious with him, let them be.** Their anger is earned, and he won't defend himself.

## Scene 6 — The Choice

### Branch A — They touch nothing and leave *(the correct play)*

No fight. No chase. Nothing happens.

They climb back up through a silent ship, cross a deck where nothing moves, and go over the rail. The Shadow sails on with no wind. The storm closes behind them, exactly as before. The compass goes inert in their hands the moment they clear the weather.

They have: their lives, their captain, a compass that no longer points at anything, and **no answers whatsoever.**

**Play the anticlimax straight.** Do not add a hazard to make it feel earned. The unsatisfying quiet *is* the reward, and letting the players sit in it — did we just do the right thing? did we waste all of that? — is the best thing this branch has. Then take them home and give them Scene 7A.

### Branch B — Someone sits *(most likely Hex)*

He climbs the ladder, crosses the quarterdeck, and lowers himself into the seat almost gently. Nothing happens for about two seconds.

> *Then he is not there. The chair does not move. Nothing falls, nothing cries out, nothing is thrown clear — a man simply stops being in a place, and a hundred feet below you every chain in the hull goes slack at once.*

Then the ship starts screaming, and **the Shadow tears itself open.**

- **Lolth's power is loose**, and it is wearing Hex.
- **Seraphel is free** — the gold orb unfolds into a man, and for one moment, in a ship coming apart around them, there is a person standing in the hold who is glowing gold and looking at his own hands like he's never seen them.
- **Run the gauntlet.**

**What steps onto the deck** is Hex — his face, his coat, his voice, his memories. It is not Hex. It is unhurried, it is pleased, and it has no interest in fighting anyone. It watches them run, and as the Stormcaller pulls away, **it raises a hand and waves.**

Then it walks off the wreck and does not sink.

## The Gauntlet *(Branch B only)*

**Lolth is not a fight.** No attack rolls against her, no HP, no initiative slot as a combatant. She is a lair-scale force that acts once per round while the party runs, and the ship coming apart is the other half of the threat. Do not let anyone roll to hit the thing wearing Hex.

**Say the clock out loud, in character, before round 1.** Maren, from the Stormcaller: *"Five minutes, then I cut the line — I'm sorry, I'm sorry, five minutes!"* Nobody dies to a rule they weren't told.

**Four legs, one per round, from the bottom of the hull to the Stormcaller's deck:**

| Leg | The move | Hazard |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — The hold** | Get out before the keel splits | Chains whip loose as the hull opens. **DC 14 Dex**, **3d8 bludgeoning** on a fail, half on a success. Failure also costs 10 ft. |
| **2 — The lower decks** | Climb up through a ship that is folding | Stairs are gone, decks are tilting past 40 degrees. **DC 15 Athletics or Acrobatics**; on a fail, **3d8 bludgeoning** and no progress this round. |
| **3 — The main deck** | Cross open deck under it | Lair action from the thing wearing Hex: **4d10 necrotic**, DC 15 Con for half, centered on the largest cluster of creatures. Reward anyone who spreads out. |
| **4 — Over the rail** | Longboat or swim | The Shadow is going down and pulling everything with it. **DC 14 Str (Athletics)** or be dragged 20 ft toward the wreck and take **2d8**. |

**Seraphel buys them one round.** At the start of **leg 3**, he steps between the party and the thing on the quarterdeck and holds it — gold light against violet, one man against a piece of a god, and he is visibly not strong enough anymore. Everyone gets a free round of movement. This is how they survive, and it's how he introduces himself: not with a speech, with a body between them and it.

**Tuning:**
- Aim for **one PC hitting 0 HP** in a typical run. A downed character costs another PC their movement for a leg to drag them — that's the pressure that makes the clock bite.
- **Death is on the table** for a party that splits, lingers to loot, or leaves someone behind. It should not be the expected outcome.
- Three players, levels unrecorded in the character files — **check their actual HP before the session and adjust the 3d8 / 4d10 dice up or down.** The shape matters more than the numbers.
- **Hard line, telegraphed:** anything still aboard the Shadow at the end of leg 4 does not come back.
- Give them outs that cost something: dropping the compass buys 10 ft; abandoning Joss buys a full round and should be remembered forever.

**The last image.** From the Stormcaller's deck: the storm unravelling — not closing, *unravelling*, the wall of weather that has stood for centuries coming apart like something that has been switched off. The Shadow going under. And a man in a familiar coat standing on the last of it, waving.

## Scene 7 — The Answers

### 7A — Xendros *(Branch A)*

They come home with nothing and go to the one person in Saltmarsh who deals in things she shouldn't. **She already knows.** She has known for years.

**She is not sorry, and she would never have stopped them.** She's chaotic to the bone; she thinks the whole thing is fascinating and she'd have been genuinely curious to see what happened either way. But she likes them, they owe her a magic item, and she'll talk.

Give her the full contents of **The Truth** above, in her voice: the severed piece of Lolth, the celestial who volunteered to be the container, the storm raised to bar gods and keep it moving, the chair as a mortal anchor, the captain who sat and was taken, and — the part that lands hardest — **the chart.**

**On Ilvessa, she is precise and unbothered:** a drider, one of Lolth's ruined ones, sent out to do the errands the Queen can't do herself. Xendros knows the type and possibly knows the individual. She'll note, with what is almost admiration, that thirty years is nothing to a creature like that — Hex wasn't recruited, he was *planted*, and then left to grow.

**If Hex hasn't confessed the drider by now, Xendros does it for him** — casually, without any idea she's detonating anything: *"Well, obviously. Eight legs. You knew that, Captain."* Watch his face. This is the ugliest available version of the reveal, and it's a perfectly fair one if the party never asked and he never volunteered.

**She does not know about the last thing Ilvessa said.** If a player repeats *"believe the light"* to her, Xendros stops being amused for exactly one moment. That was not part of the errand. Somebody down there broke ranks, and that is the most interesting thing she's heard in years.

End the session on Hex's face when he understands he's been walked to that ship since he was a boy — and on the knowledge that a man is still down there holding it alone, and that the way to help him is the one thing they must never do.

### 7B — Seraphel *(Branch B)*

Aboard the Stormcaller, wet, exhausted, a man glowing gold sitting on a deck he hasn't stood on in centuries.

He gives them everything in **The Truth**. Points to hit, in this order:

1. What the purple thing is, and that the sea powers couldn't destroy it.
2. That he **volunteered**. Nobody sentenced him. He chose it, and he'd choose it again, and he's been awake the entire time.
3. Why the storm — to hide it, to keep gods out, to keep it moving.
4. What the chair is, and that **Emerich Sallow** sat willingly and was taken. The first break. Seraphel watched it happen from the inside and has had a century to think about it — he'll say Sallow was a good man and that it made no difference at all.
5. That there had been no anchor for a hundred years, and he had been slipping the whole time.
6. **That she cultivated Hex.** The chart, the exile, the obsession, the blood oath, the compass. All of it was road.
7. What is loose now, and that it cannot be put back the way it was.

**On the light.** If the party tells him what Ilvessa said — *"believe the light"* — it stops him cold. He has been a sphere in the dark for centuries with no way to know that anyone out in the world ever tried to help him, and it turns out one of *her* creatures did. He will want to find Ilvessa. That want should be the first thing he asks them for.

**The note to play him on:** he is not angry with them. He is *relieved* — and deeply ashamed of being relieved, because his relief cost the world something enormous. He will not say he forgives them, because from his side there is nothing to forgive and he knows they need to hear it anyway. That tension is the character.

He stays. He's the campaign's new ally and the only one who understands the enemy.
